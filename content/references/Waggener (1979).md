## Abstract
This paper presents the results of a 1977 survey of students who completed the policy course (graduate and undergraduate). Its purpose is twofold: (I) to analyze students’ perception of the learning techniques used in the courses and (2) to display the results so instructors can compare conceptual (text and readings) with experiential (cases and simulation) techniques as students see them. The study shows that students perceive experiential techniques to be more effective and more enjoyable (also more time-consuming) than conceptual techniques, except in providing an understanding of top management problems. Of the experiential techniques, simulation was preferred to cases, except in the area of problem solving where cases were recognized as valuable. Results from graduate and undergraduate students followed similar patterns, but a greater percentage of graduate students felt that conceptual techniques were important.
